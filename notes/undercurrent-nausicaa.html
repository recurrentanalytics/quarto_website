Nausicaä, on paper

under:current
Why Miyazaki’s manga is worth reading even if you know the film, without giving anything away.
Published

November 20, 2026

Modified

November 25, 2026

If you know the film, you know the overture. Read the manga anyway. No spoilers here.

Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind ran in Tokuma Shoten’s Animage magazine from 1982 to 1994, twelve years with long pauses while Miyazaki made films, and fills seven volumes. The 1984 film came out when only the first sixteen chapters existed and covers roughly the first two volumes. The rest grew over the next ten years into something much larger and darker than the film.

The premise is simple. A world poisoned long ago. A toxic forest that keeps spreading, full of giant insects. People living at its edge, in small valleys and under empires, afraid of it. And a princess who goes into the forest to study it instead of fighting it, because she wants to understand it.

That is the part I love most. The forest is drawn as a living world, not a monster: strange, deadly, and endlessly interesting. Fear is the normal response in that world, and curiosity is the unusual one. The book takes the side of curiosity without pretending it is safe.

Then the drawing. Miyazaki drew the pages mostly in pencil, without inking, and the collected volumes are printed in brown ink. The pages are dense, almost crowded: machines, armour, clouds, crowds, plants, all in fine pencil lines, filling the panels to their edges. Next to the clean colour of the film, they look like field notes from a world that kept on existing after the camera left.

And the politics. The empires around the valley are not good or evil. Everyone has reasons, most of them bad, some of them understandable, and the story does not tidy them up for you. It is a book about people with reasons.

Some neighbours. The heroine has two sources Miyazaki named himself: the princess Nausicaa of the Odyssey, as he met her in Bernard Evslin’s small dictionary of Greek mythology, and The Lady Who Loved Insects, a Heian-period Japanese tale about a young noblewoman who would rather keep caterpillars than behave as expected. The tale is short, and worth finding.

For the drawing, the natural companion is Moebius, the French artist Jean Giraud. He and Miyazaki shared an exhibition at the Monnaie de Paris from December 2004 to March 2005, and the pairing makes sense on the page. For the forest, a much later relative: Jeff VanderMeer’s Annihilation (2014), in which a small expedition walks into Area X, a stretch of coast that nature has taken back and nobody understands. Both books ask how to look at a living place that will not behave like scenery.

Read it slowly. It rewards looking at each page longer than you need to follow the story.
