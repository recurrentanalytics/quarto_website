Eighty-nine papers and a sandpile

under:current
The Santa Fe Institute’s four-volume Foundational Papers in Complexity Science: the climate book hidden inside it, two favourites and an argument.
Published

November 20, 2026

Modified

November 24, 2026

This one is long, because the books are.

The object

Foundational Papers in Complexity Science is a set of four volumes from SFI Press, the Santa Fe Institute’s publisher, edited by David C. Krakauer and published over the course of 2024. It reprints 89 papers written between 1922 and 2000, each with a new introduction and annotations by present-day researchers. By the publisher’s page counts that is a little over 3,100 pages.

It is not a set you read from front to back. It is one you open somewhere in the middle and argue with.

The order is strictly chronological, and I think that is the best editorial decision in it. Papers that were never meant to meet end up as neighbours. Hayek’s “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945) sits two places before Shannon’s “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” (1948): one on how prices carry what no planner can know, the other on how any message can be counted in bits. Turing’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950) is followed by Nash’s “Non-Cooperative Games” (1951) and then by Turing again, two years on, asking how pattern in a living thing could arise from chemistry alone. You would never put these on one syllabus. Side by side they look like one long conversation about how order comes out of many small parts that do not know about the whole.

The climate book hidden inside

Nobody would sell this as a climate book. Read the contents with climate in mind, though, and one appears.

  • Lorenz, “Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow” (1963). Three equations for convection whose solutions never settle and never repeat. Two almost identical starting points drift apart until they have nothing in common. This is why a weather forecast has a horizon, and why climate has to be asked about in a different way from weather.
  • Mandelbrot, “How Long is the Coast of Britain?” (1967). The measured length of a coast depends on the length of the ruler. Every dataset of exposed coastline should carry that sentence on its first page, and almost none does.
  • Budyko, “The Effect of Solar Radiation Variations on the Climate of the Earth” (1969). An energy balance model with ice in it. Ice reflects sunlight, the cooling grows more ice, and past a point the feedback runs away towards a frozen planet. Tipping points, decades before the phrase became common.
  • May, “Will a Large Complex System Be Stable?” (1972). Two pages in Nature. Make a system larger and more richly connected at random, and beyond a threshold it becomes almost certainly unstable. The intuition that more connections mean more safety is not a law.
  • Holling, “Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems” (1973). Holling separates stability, how quickly a system returns to where it was, from resilience, how much disturbance it can take and still persist as the same kind of system. Much of today’s talk of resilience in adaptation plans quietly blurs the two.
  • Hasselmann, “Stochastic Climate Models Part I. Theory” (1976). Weather treated as random noise that the slow ocean adds up into climate variability. Hasselmann shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics for the physical modelling of Earth’s climate. The other half of that prize went to Giorgio Parisi, whose 1979 spin glass paper sits a few entries further on in the same volume. I find that funnier than I can justify.
  • Lansing and Kremer, “Emergent Properties of Balinese Water Temple Networks” (1993). The irrigation of a whole landscape coordinated through a network of water temples, and modelled as a system whose good outcome is a property of the network, not of any one farmer or any one plan.
  • Ostrom, “Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms” (2000). The last paper in the set, on how groups manage shared things without either a state or a market doing it for them. It is a good place to end a century.

Put together, this is most of what I would want someone to have in their head before they build a climate risk model: limits to prediction, measures that depend on scale, thresholds, fragile connectivity, two meanings of resilience, noise that adds up, and institutions that work without a centre.

Two favourites

The sandpile. Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld, “Self-Organized Criticality: An Explanation of the 1/f Noise” (1987). Drop grains of sand onto a pile one at a time. The pile builds itself to a critical slope and then stays there, shedding avalanches of every size: many small ones, a few huge ones, with nobody tuning anything. What I love in it is the image, and one uncomfortable consequence: the grain that starts a catastrophe is an ordinary grain. The size of the trigger says nothing about the size of what follows. How much of the real world works like that is still argued about, and I do not think the argument is over. As a picture for risk it is hard to beat.

The best title. Conant and Ashby, “Every Good Regulator of a System Must Be a Model of That System” (1970). I have nothing to add to the title except that it should hang above every desk where someone manages a risk with a model they do not understand.

A smaller pleasure: Craig Reynolds’ “Flocks, Herds, and Schools: A Distributed Behavioral Model” (1987) is in volume three, three rules that make a flock without a leader. The fish in the mangrove toy swim by those rules, as its taken-apart page explains.

Neighbours outside the books

The set has no music in it, but complexity has a soundtrack. Steve Reich’s Music for 18 Musicians (1976): pulses and short repeated patterns for many players, and a large shape that changes so slowly you hear it arrive rather than watch it being built. Brian Eno’s Ambient 1: Music for Airports from the end of the 1970s, built from tape loops of different lengths that drift in and out of step, so the music keeps making combinations no one wrote down.

Then a game with no players: John Conway’s Game of Life, introduced to the world in Martin Gardner’s “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American in October 1970. A grid, a handful of rules that fit on a napkin, and behaviour people are still cataloguing.

And on stage, Tom Stoppard’s Arcadia (1993), a country-house comedy whose scenes move between two centuries in the same room, with iterated equations, heat and the second law of thermodynamics in among the gossip. It is the only play I know in which an algorithm gets a laugh.

An argument with it

My quarrel is with what the set leaves out by design. Most of the papers here are models or theory, and the best are beautiful because they are so spare: a sandpile, a random matrix, three equations. What the century of complexity science did much less often, at least in this selection, was take one of those models to a messy record of the real world and see what survived. For climate risk that confrontation is the whole job. The gap between a sandpile and a loss database is where the work is, and those volumes mostly stop at its edge.

The other quarrel is gentler. The chronological order that I praised above also hides the threads. A reader who wants to follow one idea, feedback, say, or networks, has to build the path through the volumes alone. Perhaps that is the point. It is certainly how I would want to read it anyway: with a pencil, out of order, and with a list of papers to go back to.

What I take from it

Complexity is not a method. It is a set of warnings, each with a name and a year attached, and the most useful ones are about where our models stop. Reading the warnings in their original form, one paper at a time, is a better use of an evening than most new papers.
