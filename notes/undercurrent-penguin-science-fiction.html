A shelf of strange books

A series, not a single work, and a good argument for letting a publisher choose for you now and then.

The series

Penguin Science Fiction launched in August 2020 under the Penguin Classics imprint, with ten books and more following. The publisher’s series page lists nineteen as of November 2026. Penguin said it wanted to challenge stereotypes about the genre, and for once the list actually does it.

What strikes me is the mix. Flatland (1884) and Vonnegut’s Cat’s Cradle are the names you expect. Around them sit books that are much less known in English: Japanese, Russian, Polish, German and Argentinian science fiction in translation, a 1931 satire on race in America, cosmic mysticism, pulp horror, robot fairy tales. Very little of it is about spaceships. Much of it is funny. A lot of it is about catastrophe, and almost none of it treats catastrophe as an action film. These are books about what people do while the world goes wrong slowly, absurdly or in plain sight.

Four to start with, chosen for range. Premises only.

Kobo Abe, The Ark Sakura

The shelter is ready: a disused quarry, fitted out by one man who goes by Mole, for the day the bombs fall. What it still lacks is a crew. Abe published it in Japanese in 1984; the English translation by Juliet Winters Carpenter followed in 1988, and that is the one Penguin prints.

It is the best book I know about preparedness as a state of mind. The shelter is enormous, carefully engineered and slightly ridiculous, and its builder is all three as well. Anyone who has ever planned for the worst will recognise something uncomfortable in him, and laugh anyway. If it hooks you, go back to Abe’s The Woman in the Dunes (1962) and Hiroshi Teshigahara’s film of it (1964), for which Abe wrote the screenplay: an insect collector, a village in the sand, and a premise I will leave there.

Harry Harrison, Make Room! Make Room!

Written in 1966 and set in a New York of thirty-five million people at the end of the twentieth century, where water and food are rationed and everything is shared with too many others. A police detective is the thread through it.

What I like is how unspectacular it is. No single disaster, only scarcity as weather: queues, crowding, the daily arithmetic of not enough. As a forecast it got the numbers wrong. As a picture of a system running at its limit for years, it still reads well. It is in the series too.

Stanisław Lem, The Cyberiad

Two robot engineers, Trurl and Klapaucius, build machines for kings, for each other and for bets, across a universe that seems to be made mostly of robots. Lem published the collection in Polish in 1965; Michael Kandel’s English translation came out in 1974.

These are fairy tales for engineers, and the jokes are engineering jokes, about specifications, precision and pride. Lem’s wordplay is everywhere, and Kandel’s English version of it is a small miracle of its own. Read it for the pleasure of the language before anything else, with Kraftwerk’s Die Mensch-Maschine (1978) playing: robots, deadpan, and fonder of people than they let on. Penguin edition.

Arkady and Boris Strugatsky, One Billion Years to the End of the World

Leningrad in high summer. Malianov, an astrophysicist, has the calculation of his life on his desk, and the world will not leave him alone with it. That is as far as I will go.

The Strugatskys published it in Russian in 1976 and 1977, in a magazine; Antonina W. Bouis translated it, first under the English title Definitely Maybe (1978), and Penguin’s edition credits her translation, under a title closer to the original. It is a short book about pressure, and I think it is the best of the four. Their Roadside Picnic (1972) is not in this series, but it is the other door into their work: it became Andrei Tarkovsky’s Stalker (1979), which the brothers scripted, and book and film are different enough that both are worth having.

Where to go next

The rest of the list rewards wandering: Tiptree’s two collections, Gorodischer’s Trafalgar, Eschbach’s The Hair Carpet Weavers, Schuyler’s Black No More. Pick by title. That is what a series like this is for.
