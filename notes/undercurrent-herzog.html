Ice, fire, jungle, voice

under:current
Werner Herzog’s films as one body of work: landscapes at their limit and the people who go there. No spoilers.
Published

November 21, 2026

Modified

November 25, 2026

Not one film this time but all of them, or as many as I have caught. Werner Herzog, born in Munich in 1942, has made more than sixty features and documentaries, and in 2025 Venice gave him its Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement. What holds them together for me is not a genre. It is a landscape at its limit, a person who goes there anyway, and a voice that refuses to pretend the landscape cares.

Jungle

The early fiction is where most people start. In Aguirre, the Wrath of God (1972), conquistadors go down from the Andes into the Amazon in search of El Dorado. In Fitzcarraldo (1982), which won him Best Director at Cannes, a man who wants to bring opera to the jungle sets out to haul a steamship over a mountain between two rivers. That is the premise, and I will leave it there.

Both are scored by Popol Vuh, Florian Fricke’s group. Their Aguirre album is worth hearing on its own, without the pictures, in the dark.

Fire

Lessons of Darkness (1992) films the oil fires of Kuwait after the Gulf War, shortly before they were put out, as if they were the landscape of another planet. It is the most disturbing climate film I know, and it never mentions climate.

The volcano films came later. Into the Inferno (2016) he co-directed with the volcanologist Clive Oppenheimer. The Fire Within (2022) is built from more than two hundred hours of footage shot by the volcanologists Katia and Maurice Krafft. In the same year Sara Dosa made Fire of Love from the same archive. Watching both is a lesson in how much the cut decides: same people, same mountains, two different films.

Ice

Encounters at the End of the World (2007) goes to McMurdo Station in Antarctica and finds, among the ice, the caves and the volcanoes, a community of people who ended up at the bottom of the world for reasons of their own. It is the warmest film he made, and the one I would start with.

People at the edge

Grizzly Man (2005) is built largely from the footage of Timothy Treadwell, who filmed himself among the bears of Katmai in Alaska. Cave of Forgotten Dreams (2010) takes a 3D camera into the Chauvet cave in southern France. And in Ghost Elephants (2025), Steve Boyes searches the highlands of Angola for a herd that may or may not be there.

The voice

In 1999 Herzog read out his twelve-point Minnesota Declaration, a short polemic against the documentary that only records facts. Its central claim is that “there is such a thing as poetic, ecstatic truth”. You can disagree with it, and I sometimes do. But it explains the films better than any review.

He also writes. The Twilight World, his first novel (German 2021, English 2022), is about Hiroo Onoda on the island of Lubang. His memoir came out in German in 2022 as Jeder für sich und Gott gegen alle and in English in 2023. Both read like the films sound.
