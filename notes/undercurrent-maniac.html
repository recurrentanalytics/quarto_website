MANIAC, twice

under:current
Benjamín Labatut’s novel about John von Neumann and the machines that came after him, in English and in his own Spanish. Premise only.
Published

November 20, 2026

Modified

November 25, 2026

Three parts, like the book, then three neighbours and a detour.

One book, two languages

Benjamín Labatut is Chilean, and his earlier book, Un verdor terrible, was written in Spanish and translated into English by Adrian Nathan West as When We Cease to Understand the World. With this one he turned it around. He wrote The MANIAC in English, his first book in that language; it came out with Penguin Press in New York on 3 October 2023, and with Pushkin Press in Britain. Then he translated it into Spanish himself. Anagrama published that version as MANIAC on 25 October 2023, in its series for Spanish-language literature; its page names no other translator.

That is rarer than it sounds. A translator owes the original loyalty. An author translating himself owes it nothing, and at a press conference in Barcelona in November 2023 he said as much: translating himself let him betray himself in a way no translator would. So, in a sense, there are two originals, and the second is allowed to depart from the first. I like that idea more than I can say, and it suits a book about people who did not trust a single version of anything. If you read both languages, read a chapter or two side by side. Not to catch differences, but to watch one writer choose twice.

The premise

The three parts carry first names: Paul, John, Lee. Paul is the physicist Paul Ehrenfest. John is von Neumann, the longest part, made of many voices. Lee is Lee Sedol, at a Go board in Seoul in 2016, across from AlphaGo. That is all I will say about what happens. The history is public; the book’s way through it is worth arriving at fresh.

Be clear about what kind of book it is. In interviews Labatut has said his details are usually real, and that he goes close to the record and then steps back. The voices in the middle are his writing, an imagined oral history of real people, not transcripts, and reviewers have noted that he takes liberties with the lives. Read it as a novel that keeps close to the record, not as a biography.

Three neighbours

George Dyson’s Turing’s Cathedral (2012) is the non-fiction history of von Neumann’s computer at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton: the record that the novel goes close to and then steps back from. Greg Kohs’ documentary AlphaGo (2017) follows the people who built the program up to and through the match in Seoul. And Philip Glass and Robert Wilson’s Einstein on the Beach (1976), an opera about a scientist that tells no story about him at all, built from repetition, counted numbers and images. It is the opposite of Labatut’s method, and seeing both makes each clearer.

The machine, and a tangent

The MANIAC itself was a computer built at Los Alamos in the early 1950s under Nicholas Metropolis, modelled on the machine von Neumann was building at Princeton. It sits in a line of work that matters to anyone who models risk. In 1946 Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from an illness and playing solitaire, wondered whether he could estimate the odds of winning by simply playing many random games instead of doing the combinatorics. With von Neumann, Metropolis and Robert Richtmyer that became the Monte Carlo method, named by Metropolis partly after an uncle of Ulam’s who liked the casino. Its first runs, on ENIAC in 1948, were bomb physics.

Every catastrophe model that simulates ten thousand years of storms, every probabilistic loss curve, every basis risk estimate on a synthetic event set, descends from that idea: when the equations are too hard, roll the dice many times and count. The book is not about Monte Carlo. But it is about the people in whose rooms it was born, what they built, and what they thought they were doing. That makes it, to me, an uncomfortable family history of the tools risk modelling still uses.
