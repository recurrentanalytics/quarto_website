Port Augusta, taken apart

How a real place and one real day became a scene in the browser, piece by piece

showcase
energy
remote sensing
WebGL
The Port Augusta energy diorama, taken apart: five-minute market data, a terrain from open heights, a satellite picture of the day before, houses from open outlines, light and shadow in WebGL and a small battery game, with code, diagrams and a reading list.
Published

November 21, 2026

The Port Augusta scene shows the head of Spencer Gulf in South Australia on one day, 16 October 2026: two solar farms, two wind farms and a battery, moving as the market operator’s five-minute data say they did, under the sun of that day. This article takes it apart: what each piece does, the code that does it, the idea behind it, and what to read to go further.

NoteHow this was made

Built with AI coding tools: I set the direction, tested and decided; the tools wrote the code and the first draft of this article. The reference list was checked against Crossref, and what I say about each source against its abstract or an open copy. I am not an expert in this field. I am learning it, and this article is my map so far.

A real place, and what is drawn all the same

The mangrove toy on this site is made up from the first rule to the last. This scene is the opposite case. The plants’ output, the price, the heights, the ground’s colour, the shore, the building outlines and the sun’s position are data.

Some things are still drawn, and the scene’s own info panel lists them. The hills are three times higher than life, the turbines about five times larger and the houses half again as high, so that they can be seen from 40 km away. Roof shapes, walls, windows and most building heights are made up. The sky has no clouds and no weather. The glow of a solar field and the turning of a rotor show a share of the plant’s capacity, not what a camera would see. The fine structure of the ground close up is from pictures taken between 2008 and 2010. Each part below says where the data end and the drawing begins.

The shape of the program

The page is one HTML file of about 150 KB with no library. A short Python script puts it together from nine JavaScript modules, a page template and a style sheet; the modules have about 2,400 lines. Drawing is WebGL2 written by hand. Next to the page lie its data files: about 8 MB on a first visit, and 18 MB more of fine ground detail that is fetched only when the view comes close. Five Python scripts, about 1,300 lines, make those files from open data; they run once, ahead of time, not in the browser.

Every frame, the page turns the time of day into a handful of numbers that everything else reads: how much each solar farm and each wind farm gives as a share of its capacity, and what the battery does (from main.js):

    const target = {
      solar: [share(['BNGSF1', 'BNGSF2'], S.tm), share(['PAREPS1'], S.tm)],
      wind: [share(['LGAPWF1', 'LGAPWF2'], S.tm), share(['PAREPW1'], S.tm)],
    };
    const bmw = G.on ? G.mw : tl.mw('LGAPBS1', S.tm) || 0, breg = day.units.LGAPBS1.registration;   // in the game: the visitor's battery
    const bat = clamp(bmw < 0 ? bmw / breg.max_cap_cons_mw : bmw / breg.max_cap_gen_mw, -1, 1);
    const snap = !animate || S.scrub;
    for (let f = 0; f < 2; f++) {
      sm.solar[f] = snap ? target.solar[f] : lerp(sm.solar[f], target.solar[f], k);
      sm.wind[f] = snap ? target.wind[f] : lerp(sm.wind[f], target.wind[f], k);
      if (animate) rot[f] -= sm.wind[f] * 1.5 * dt;
    }
    sm.bat = snap ? bat : lerp(sm.bat, bat, k);

sm is the smoothed state the drawing uses, so that a jump in the data becomes a short glide in the picture. G belongs to the battery game further down.

The market, five minutes at a time

Most of eastern and southern Australia shares one wholesale market for electricity, the National Electricity Market. South Australia is one of its regions. The market operator, AEMO, publishes two things that this scene is built on: for the generating units and batteries it dispatches (and others it has readings for) a power reading every five minutes, and for every region a price for every five-minute interval. The scene uses seven units (two for Bungala solar, two for Lincoln Gap wind, one battery, one each for wind and solar at the Port Augusta Renewable Energy Park) and the price of the South Australian region, 288 values each for the day.

A reading is a moment, not an average. Between two readings the scene draws a straight line (from data.js):

  tl.mw = (duid, tm) => {
    const a = day.units[duid].mw;
    const f = clamp(tm / step, 0, n - 1);
    const i = Math.floor(f), j = Math.min(n - 1, i + 1);
    const x = a[i], y = a[j];
    if (x == null) return y == null ? null : y;
    if (y == null) return x;
    return x + (y - x) * (f - i);
  };

The price is treated differently: it holds for its whole interval and jumps at the end.

On the replayed day the price ran from −10.81 to 152.01 Australian dollars per megawatt hour, and it was below zero in 68 of the 288 intervals, all between 07:45 and 13:30 local time. A price below zero means that a generator selling at the spot price pays to feed in, and whoever takes power is paid. The data do not say why the price fell on this day, and the scene does not claim to know. What is known in general is that the market value of wind and solar power falls as their share grows (Hirth 2013), and that wind generation was already found to move South Australian spot prices more than a decade ago (Cutler et al. 2011). Simshauser (2018) discusses what a rising share of intermittent generation means for the stability of this market.

Choosing the day

The first version replayed 20 November 2026. On that day both units of the Port Augusta Renewable Energy Park stood at 0 MW in every interval, so two of the four farms did not move. A look through the operator’s daily archive showed that they had read 0 MW since 20 October. The data do not say why. I asked for a day on which the park runs, and the scan of the archive gave 16 October: the park’s wind farm ran all day, the solar farms ran through the day, the battery both charged and discharged, and the price went below zero in the morning.

Picking a day is a choice, and it shapes the picture. A scene like this shows one day well and says nothing about the other 364.

The sun

The light follows the real sun. Its height and direction come from the date, the time and the place, with the short formulas of the kind the Astronomical Almanac gives for an approximate solar position (Michalsky 1988) (from data.js):

function sunPosition(utcMs, latDeg, lonDeg) {
  const rad = Math.PI / 180;
  const d = utcMs / 86400000 + 2440587.5 - 2451545.0; // days since J2000.0
  const g = (357.529 + 0.98560028 * d) * rad;
  const q = 280.459 + 0.98564736 * d;
  const L = (q + 1.915 * Math.sin(g) + 0.020 * Math.sin(2 * g)) * rad;
  const e = (23.439 - 0.00000036 * d) * rad;
  const ra = Math.atan2(Math.cos(e) * Math.sin(L), Math.cos(L));
  const dec = Math.asin(Math.sin(e) * Math.sin(L));
  const gmst = (18.697374558 + 24.06570982441908 * d) % 24;
  const ha = ((gmst * 15 + lonDeg) * rad) - ra;
  const lat = latDeg * rad;
  const el = Math.asin(Math.sin(lat) * Math.sin(dec) + Math.cos(lat) * Math.cos(dec) * Math.cos(ha));
  const az = Math.atan2(-Math.sin(ha), Math.tan(dec) * Math.cos(lat) - Math.sin(lat) * Math.cos(ha));
  return { el, az: (az + 2 * Math.PI) % (2 * Math.PI) };
}

The result is good to about a degree, which is plenty for light and shadow. The colours of sky and sunlight are then read from small hand-made tables by the sun’s height; they are drawn, not measured.

The ground

The heights are Geoscience Australia’s one-arc-second elevation model, about 30 m between points, which goes back to the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (Farr et al. 2007). For the mesh the scene averages them to 297 by 180 cells of about 210 by 250 m and draws them three times higher than life. The coastline, roads, railway and power lines are from OpenStreetMap (Haklay and Weber 2008), thinned with the line-simplification method of Douglas and Peucker (1973).

A mesh of 210 m cells is too coarse to show a ridge. So the light does not use the mesh’s slopes but the slopes of the full 30 m heights, stored as a picture: two colour channels hold the direction the ground faces, the third how open a spot is (from diorama_imagery.py):

gz, gx = np.gradient(hs, dz, dx)
n = np.dstack([-gx * VEX, np.ones_like(hs), -gz * VEX])
n /= np.linalg.norm(n, axis=2, keepdims=True)
# openness: height against its surroundings (about 400 m around), 0.5 = level ground
op = np.clip(0.5 + (hs - blur(hs, 13.0)) / 70.0, 0, 1)

Lighting a coarse surface with finer slopes kept in a picture goes back to Blinn (1978). The third channel dims the light from the sky a little in valleys and raises it on ridges.

Shadows of the hills

When the sun is low, the Flinders Ranges throw long shadows over the plain. The scene works them out on the processor, not on the graphics chip: from every ground cell it walks toward the sun over the drawn heights and checks whether the ground rises above the ray (from main.js):

          let x = gx, z = gy, ray = HY[i] + 0.004, v = 1;
          for (let k = 1; k <= maxSteps; k++) {
            x += dx; z += dz; ray += dh;
            if (ray > maxHY || x < 0 || z < 0 || x >= nx - 1 || z >= ny - 1) break;
            // the ground under the ray, between the four cells around it
            const ix = x | 0, iz = z | 0, fx = x - ix, fz = z - iz, o = iz * nx + ix;
            const g = (HY[o] * (1 - fx) + HY[o + 1] * fx) * (1 - fz) + (HY[o + nx] * (1 - fx) + HY[o + nx + 1] * fx) * fz;
            const d = g - ray;
            if (d > 0) {
              const t = d / (0.006 + 0.05 * k * step);
              if (t >= 1) { v = 0; break; }
              if (1 - t < v) v = 1 - t;
            }
          }
          out[i] = v * 255;

The walk is redone when the sun has moved more than 0.4 degrees, 24 rows of cells per frame, so that it never holds up a frame. Because the hills are drawn three times higher, their shadows are longer than in life. The two classic ways to draw shadows on a graphics chip, shadow volumes and shadow maps, go back to Crow (1977) and Williams (1978); for a single height field and one sun, the walk above is simpler than either.

The satellite picture

The ground’s colour is a picture from the Sentinel-2B satellite (Drusch et al. 2012), taken on 15 October 2026 at about 11:30 local time, one day before the replayed day, with no clouds over the area. I use it as Geoscience Australia publishes it in Digital Earth Australia (Dhu et al. 2017): corrected for the atmosphere, for the angles of sun and view and for the slope of the ground, a method described for Landsat pictures in Li et al. (2012). What arrives is not a photograph but, for every 10 m of ground, the share of red, green and blue light the surface reflects. One line turns that into a colour for the eye, the same for all three channels (from diorama_imagery.py):

disp = np.clip(rgb * 3.2, 0, 1) ** (1 / 2.0)      # the same exposure and gamma as ground.jpg

The picture carries the light of its own moment: shadows in it point where the sun stood at 11:30. The scene mixes that with its drawn light of the hour, which is a compromise, not a correction.

Out to the horizon

At first the scene was a block of 62 by 44 km standing in empty space, and on a phone it looked like a brown slab. The same satellite pass covers far more, so the land now runs on to the horizon in three rings of falling detail.

The shader for the wider land looks up the coarse picture everywhere and lays the sharper one over it where it exists (from shaders.js):

  vec2 um = vP.xz / (uOuterBox * 0.5) + 0.5;
  vec2 em = abs(um - 0.5);
  float inMid = uMidMix * (1.0 - smoothstep(0.47, 0.5, max(em.x, em.y)));
  picture = mix(picture, texture(uMid, um).rgb, inMid);
  mask = mix(mask, texture(uMidWater, um).r, inMid);

Far out, the land fades into the colour the sky has at the horizon in that direction, so there is no edge to see:

  float far = smoothstep(50.0, 106.0, length(vP.xz));
  vec3 v = normalize(vP - uEye);
  o = vec4(mix(fogged(c, vP), tone(horizonAt(v) + veilAt(v)), far), uOuterMix);

Rings of detail that get coarser with distance are the idea of geometry clipmaps (Losasso and Hoppe 2004). There the rings follow the viewer; here they are fixed around the place, which is enough for a scene one looks at from all sides but does not travel through.

Where the water is

The first shore was the OpenStreetMap coastline on the 210 m mesh, which gave steps. The satellite sees water directly: water reflects some green light and almost no near infrared. The normalized difference of the two is a water index that has been in use since McFeeters (1996) (from diorama_imagery.py):

green, nir = band_mosaic("nbart_green"), band_mosaic(NIR)
index = (green - nir) / np.maximum(green + nir, 1e-4)
water = (index > 0.0) & (nir < 0.12)

The scene keeps the result only on low ground, so that a dark hill shadow cannot count as water, and draws moving water exactly there: the gulf, the river arm through the town, a few ponds. The shore is therefore the shore of that satellite moment, at whatever the tide was then.

Fine detail, from an older picture

Ten to fifteen metres per pixel is coarse when the view comes close to a street. I found no openly licensed colour picture of this area at a finer scale. There is an older black-and-white one: Geoscience Australia’s reference image at 2.5 m, put together from pictures taken between 2006 and 2010; the mosaic used here is from 2008 to 2010.

It is older than the wind and solar farms, so it cannot replace the picture of 2026. But most of what is fine in a landscape changes slowly: creek lines, rock ledges, the street grid. So the scene takes from the old picture only what is finer than about 10 m. Each tile holds the picture divided by its own blur (from diorama_detail.py):

soft = blur(pic, BLUR_PX)
ratio = np.where(soft > 14, pic / np.maximum(soft, 1.0), 1.0)    # dark water and no-data: no detail

Close up, the shader multiplies the colour of 2026 by that pattern of lighter and darker (from shaders.js):

  if (fine > 0.0) sat *= mix(1.0, uDetRange.x + uDetRange.y * dv / max(k0 + k1 + k2 + k3, 1e-3), fine);

Putting the fine structure of a sharp black-and-white picture onto a coarser colour picture is called pansharpening; Vivone et al. (2015) compare the methods. What I do is a crude relative of it: the two pictures are from different satellites and about sixteen to eighteen years apart. Where something was built in between, the fine structure is wrong. On the solar farms the scene therefore leaves it out, and the info panel says so for the rest.

The old picture comes as one file of 410 GB in a map projection, the transverse Mercator (Karney 2011), and the script reads only the rows and columns it needs. The scene’s box is cut into 6 by 5 tiles of 4096 by 3520 pixels; the 11 tiles with the town and the plants are built, 18 MB in all, and the page fetches tiles only when the view is closer than 16 km to the ground it looks at.

Houses

OpenStreetMap has 558 buildings in the area, a small part of the town. Most houses come from a second open source: outlines that a model at Microsoft found in satellite and aerial pictures. That gives 11,204 more. Model output has errors: some houses are missing, some are merged, some sit a little off.

Where both sources have the same building it must be drawn once. Two outlines count as the same when the centre of one lies inside the other, or when they cover more than 30 % of the smaller one; the cover is measured by sampling (from diorama_buildings.py):

def share(a, b, ba, bb, n: int = 24) -> float:
    """Overlap of two outlines as a share of the smaller one (sampled on a grid)."""
    x0, y0, x1, y1 = max(ba[0], bb[0]), max(ba[1], bb[1]), min(ba[2], bb[2]), min(ba[3], bb[3])
    if x1 <= x0 or y1 <= y0:
        return 0.0
    hits = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            p = (x0 + (i + 0.5) * (x1 - x0) / n, y0 + (j + 0.5) * (y1 - y0) / n)
            hits += inside(p, a) and inside(p, b)
    return hits * (x1 - x0) * (y1 - y0) / n / n / min(ring_area(a), ring_area(b))

In the page every outline is raised into a house on its true outline and drawn half again as high. Small, nearly rectangular outlines get a hipped roof, and for that the code needs the best-fitting rectangle. It tries the direction of every edge and keeps the smallest (from scene.js):

  const fitBox = (P) => {
    let best = null;
    for (let i = 0; i < P.length; i++) {
      const a = P[i], b = P[(i + 1) % P.length], len = Math.hypot(b[0] - a[0], b[1] - a[1]);
      if (len < 0.5) continue;
      const ux = (b[0] - a[0]) / len, uz = (b[1] - a[1]) / len;
      let s0 = 1e9, s1 = -1e9, t0 = 1e9, t1 = -1e9;
      for (const q of P) { const sv = q[0] * ux + q[1] * uz, tv = -q[0] * uz + q[1] * ux; s0 = Math.min(s0, sv); s1 = Math.max(s1, sv); t0 = Math.min(t0, tv); t1 = Math.max(t1, tv); }
      const ar = (s1 - s0) * (t1 - t0);
      if (!best || ar < best.ar) best = { ar, ux, uz, s0, s1, t0, t1 };
    }

For a convex outline the smallest rectangle around it does have one side along one of its edges (Freeman and Shapira 1975); for an outline with a notch, trying its edges is a good guess, not a proof. The other buildings get a flat roof on their true outline, cut into triangles by snipping off one “ear” after another, which works because every simple polygon has ears (Meisters 1975).

The Microsoft data also carry an estimated height. Here its median is 1.0 m, less than a door, so the page uses it only from 3 m up and makes up the rest by size and kind. Walls, windows and doors are a pattern drawn in the shader; the roofs take part of their colour from the satellite picture at that spot.

Turbines, panels, battery

The 109 turbines stand where OpenStreetMap maps them, with hub height and rotor size from the same source, drawn about five times larger than life. A rotor turns faster the more its farm gives, as a share of the farm’s maximum capacity in the operator’s registration list. That is a drawing of output. It is not the real rotor speed, and the direction the rotors face is not in the data.

Bungala’s panels are listed as tracking in the registration list, so the scene turns the rows with the sun, drawn as single-axis trackers; the rows are several times wider and farther apart than real ones. A field’s glow shows its output share. The battery is eight containers; their number is a drawing, not a count. It glows blue while it takes power in and amber while it gives power out.

Light, haze, water and glow

A handful of small ideas make the picture read as a landscape and not as a diagram.

Highlights that roll off. Bright ground under a high sun would clip to white. A short curve leaves everything below a knee as it is and bends what is above toward 1 (from shaders.js):

vec3 tone(vec3 c) {
  vec3 over = max(c - 0.6, 0.0);
  return min(c, 0.6) + 0.4 * (1.0 - exp(-over * 2.5));
}

Mapping a wide range of light into the narrow range of a screen is the subject of Reinhard et al. (2002); my curve is a small stand-in for what they describe.

Water. Water mirrors the sky at flat angles and shows its own colour when one looks straight down. The share of mirror follows Schlick’s approximation (Schlick 1994); the water’s own colour is the satellite picture’s (from shaders.js):

  float fr = 0.03 + 0.97 * pow(1.0 - max(dot(n, v), 0.0), 5.0);
  vec3 deep = vec3(0.05, 0.22, 0.30);
  // the water's own colour from the satellite picture: shallows, channels, tidal flats
  deep = mix(deep, picture * 0.8, pictureMix);
  deep = mix(deep, vec3(0.01, 0.03, 0.06), uNight);
  // it is lit by the sky and by the sun's brightness, not its tint
  float sunLum = dot(uSunCol, vec3(0.3, 0.55, 0.15));
  vec3 body = deep * (uAmb * 1.3 + vec3(sunLum) * 0.5 * (0.4 + 0.6 * gVis));
  vec3 c = mix(body, skyAt(reflect(-v, n), uHorizon, uZenith), fr * 0.75);

On top comes the sun’s glitter, a sharp highlight of the kind Phong (1975) introduced, broken up by a moving noise pattern.

Shadows of houses and turbines. Each is the object itself, slid along the sun’s ray down to the ground (from shaders.js):

  if (uMode == 1) {
    float g = textureLod(uHeight, p.xz / uBox + 0.5, 0.0).r;
    p.xz -= uSun.xz * (max(p.y - g, 0.0) / max(uSun.y, 0.2));
    p.y = textureLod(uHeight, p.xz / uBox + 0.5, 0.0).r + 0.0008;
  }

Where two such shadows overlap, the ground must not get twice as dark. A stencil lets every pixel be darkened once (from main.js):

    gl.enable(gl.STENCIL_TEST); gl.stencilFunc(gl.EQUAL, 0, 0xff); gl.stencilOp(gl.KEEP, gl.KEEP, gl.INCR);

Glow. After the frame is drawn, its brightest parts are copied, shrunk to a quarter, blurred and laid back over the picture. That gives the sun on the water and the windows at night their soft halo.

Staying fast on a phone

Load late. The page starts with the day’s numbers, the mesh and drawn colours. The satellite pictures, the fine slopes, the water outline and the houses arrive afterwards and fade in.

Load only what is near. The 2.5 m tiles are fetched when the view comes close and let go when it leaves (from main.js):

    if (cam.dist < 16) {
      // the two by two tiles around the point the view turns about
      const i0 = Math.floor((cam.target[0] + place.Wk / 2) / tw - 0.5), j0 = Math.floor((cam.target[2] + place.Hk / 2) / th - 0.5);
      for (const j of [j0, j0 + 1]) for (const i of [i0, i0 + 1]) if (inf.tiles.includes(i + '_' + j)) want.push(i + '_' + j);
    } else if (cam.dist > 26) {
      detail.slots.forEach((sl, k) => { if (sl) { gl.deleteTexture(sl.tex); detail.slots[k] = null; } });
    }

Build in pieces. Raising 11,762 outlines into about 580,000 corner points takes a moment. The page does it 600 houses at a time between frames. The hill shadows are spread over frames in the same way.

Resolution that gives way. If the frame rate stays low, the picture is drawn with fewer pixels and scaled up (from main.js):

      if (animate && now - motion.t0 > 9000 && diag.fps >= 8 && diag.fps < 27 && scale > 0.55) { if (++slow >= 2) { scale *= 0.85; slow = 0; resize(); } } else slow = 0;

The page also stops when the tab is hidden, and shows a still picture with a play button for visitors who have asked their device for less motion.

The battery game

The scene has one thing to do besides looking: run the battery yourself. The day passes in two minutes, the price line ends at “now”, and three keys decide what the battery does: charge, hold, discharge.

The rules fit in one function. Charging buys energy from the grid at the price of the interval, discharging sells it, and some is lost on the way in and on the way out (from game.js):

  function step(level, mode, price) {
    if (price == null || mode === 0) return { level, money: 0, mw: 0, mwh: 0 };
    if (mode > 0) {
      const bought = Math.max(0, Math.min(pIn * h, (cap - level) / eff));
      return { level: level + bought * eff, money: -bought * price, mw: -bought / h, mwh: bought };
    }
    const sold = Math.max(0, Math.min(pOut * h, level * eff));
    return { level: level - sold / eff, money: sold * price, mw: sold / h, mwh: sold };
  }

The size of the battery and how fast it can charge and discharge are from the operator’s registration list. That 85 % of the energy bought comes back out is my assumption, not a figure for this battery. There are no fees and no other income, the battery starts empty, and what is left in it at the end counts for nothing.

At the end the game shows three figures. The second is the best any plan with these three keys could have made, knowing every price in advance. It is found by working backwards from the end of the day: for the last interval the best choice at every level of charge is obvious, and from there each earlier interval can be solved in turn (from game.js):

    for (let i = n - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
      const cur = new Float64Array(L + 1), pick = new Int8Array(L + 1);
      for (let j = 0; j <= L; j++) {
        let top = -Infinity;
        for (const mode of [0, 1, -1]) {
          const r = step(lev(j), mode, prices[i]);
          const v = r.money + at(next, r.level);
          if (v > top + 1e-9) { top = v; pick[j] = mode; }
        }
        cur[j] = top;
      }
      choice[i] = pick;
      next = cur;
    }

This is dynamic programming (Bellman 1954). On this day the best plan makes about A$1,677; Figure 3 shows when it charges and when it sells. The third figure is what the real battery’s five-minute readings come to at the same prices, about A$20. It is an estimate from readings, not what the plant was paid, and batteries can also be paid for services that keep the grid steady, which these data do not show.

What storage can earn from price differences alone has been estimated properly, with several years of prices, for a market in the United States (Sioshansi et al. 2009) and for this one, in South Australia (McConnell, Forcey, and Sandiford 2015). The game is a what-if on one day. It is not a valuation, and nothing in it is advice.

What the scene leaves out

One day, chosen for the picture it gives. No clouds, no weather, no wind direction. Only the five plants with their own data are drawn; smaller solar plants nearby are left out. The scene says nothing about what a plant earns, why its output was low, or how reliable it is. Building heights, roofs and walls are mostly made up, and the Microsoft outlines were not checked one by one. The fine ground detail is about sixteen to eighteen years older than the colours. The hills, and with them their shadows, are three times too high. The picture is replayed, not live.

A reading guide

The market and what storage is worth. The falling market value of wind and solar (Hirth 2013); wind and prices in South Australia (Cutler et al. 2011); the stability of the market (Simshauser 2018); the value of storage (Sioshansi et al. 2009; McConnell, Forcey, and Sandiford 2015).

Pictures from orbit. The Sentinel-2 mission (Drusch et al. 2012); Digital Earth Australia (Dhu et al. 2017) and the correction behind its pictures (Li et al. 2012); water from green and near-infrared light (McFeeters 1996); pansharpening (Vivone et al. 2015).

Heights and maps. The radar mission behind the heights (Farr et al. 2007); OpenStreetMap (Haklay and Weber 2008); simplifying lines (Douglas and Peucker 1973); the transverse Mercator projection (Karney 2011); the sun’s position (Michalsky 1988).

## A real place, and what is drawn all the same

The [mangrove toy](../../playground/mangrove/) on this site is made up from the first rule
to the last. This scene is the opposite case. The plants' output, the price, the heights,
the ground's colour, the shore, the building outlines and the sun's position are data.

Some things are still drawn, and the scene's own info panel lists them. The hills are
three times higher than life, the turbines about five times larger and the houses half
again as high, so that they can be seen from 40 km away. Roof shapes, walls, windows and most building heights are made up.
The sky has no clouds and no weather. The glow of a solar field and the turning of a rotor
show a share of the plant's capacity, not what a camera would see. The fine structure of
the ground close up is from pictures taken between 2008 and 2010. Each part below says
where the data end and the drawing begins.

## The shape of the program

The page is one HTML file of about 150 KB with no library. A short Python script puts it
together from nine JavaScript modules, a page template and a style sheet; the modules have
about 2,400 lines. Drawing is WebGL2 written by hand. Next to the page lie its data files:
about 8 MB on a first visit, and 18 MB more of fine ground detail that is fetched only
when the view comes close. Five Python scripts, about 1,300 lines, make those files from
open data; they run once, ahead of time, not in the browser.

![From open data to the picture. Each script reads one or two sources and writes the files
the page loads; every frame, the page moves the clock, reads the day's values for that
moment, places the sun and draws.](fig-program.svg){#fig-program fig-alt="Diagram with three columns: open data sources, five Python scripts, the files the page loads; below, the steps of one frame"}

Every frame, the page turns the time of day into a handful of numbers that everything else
reads: how much each solar farm and each wind farm gives as a share of its capacity, and
what the battery does (from `main.js`):

```js
    const target = {
      solar: [share(['BNGSF1', 'BNGSF2'], S.tm), share(['PAREPS1'], S.tm)],
      wind: [share(['LGAPWF1', 'LGAPWF2'], S.tm), share(['PAREPW1'], S.tm)],
    };
    const bmw = G.on ? G.mw : tl.mw('LGAPBS1', S.tm) || 0, breg = day.units.LGAPBS1.registration;   // in the game: the visitor's battery
    const bat = clamp(bmw < 0 ? bmw / breg.max_cap_cons_mw : bmw / breg.max_cap_gen_mw, -1, 1);
    const snap = !animate || S.scrub;
    for (let f = 0; f < 2; f++) {
      sm.solar[f] = snap ? target.solar[f] : lerp(sm.solar[f], target.solar[f], k);
      sm.wind[f] = snap ? target.wind[f] : lerp(sm.wind[f], target.wind[f], k);
      if (animate) rot[f] -= sm.wind[f] * 1.5 * dt;
    }
    sm.bat = snap ? bat : lerp(sm.bat, bat, k);
```

`sm` is the smoothed state the drawing uses, so that a jump in the data becomes a short
glide in the picture. `G` belongs to the [battery game](#the-battery-game) further down.

## The market, five minutes at a time

Most of eastern and southern Australia shares one wholesale market for electricity, the
National Electricity Market. South Australia is one of its regions. The market operator,
AEMO, publishes two things that this scene is built on: for the generating units and
batteries it dispatches (and others it has readings for) a power reading every five
minutes, and for every region a price for every five-minute interval.
The scene uses seven units (two for Bungala solar, two for Lincoln Gap wind, one battery,
one each for wind and solar at the Port Augusta Renewable Energy Park) and the price of the
South Australian region, 288 values each for the day.

A reading is a moment, not an average. Between two readings the scene draws a straight
line (from `data.js`):

```js
  tl.mw = (duid, tm) => {
    const a = day.units[duid].mw;
    const f = clamp(tm / step, 0, n - 1);
    const i = Math.floor(f), j = Math.min(n - 1, i + 1);
    const x = a[i], y = a[j];
    if (x == null) return y == null ? null : y;
    if (y == null) return x;
    return x + (y - x) * (f - i);
  };
```

The price is treated differently: it holds for its whole interval and jumps at the end.

On the replayed day the price ran from −10.81 to 152.01 Australian dollars per megawatt
hour, and it was below zero in 68 of the 288 intervals, all between 07:45 and 13:30 local
time. A price below zero means that a generator selling at the spot price pays to feed in,
and whoever takes power is paid. The data do not say why
the price fell on this day, and the scene does not claim to know. What is known in general
is that the market value of wind and solar power falls as their share grows
[@hirth2013], and that wind generation was already found to move South Australian spot
prices more than a decade ago [@cutler2011]. @simshauser2018 discusses what a
rising share of intermittent generation means for the stability of this market.

![The day's price, with the hours in which the best possible plan for the battery would
charge and discharge, and the battery's level under that plan (see the game below).](fig-day.svg){#fig-day fig-alt="Line chart of the price over 24 hours, low and below zero from morning to early afternoon, with bands marking charging and discharging, and a second panel with the battery's level"}

## Choosing the day

The first version replayed 20 November 2026. On that day both units of the Port Augusta
Renewable Energy Park stood at 0 MW in every interval, so two of the four farms did not
move. A look through the operator's daily archive showed that they had read 0 MW since
20 October. The data do not say why. I asked for a day on which the park runs, and the scan
of the archive gave 16 October: the park's wind farm ran all day, the solar farms ran
through the day, the battery both charged and discharged, and the price went below zero in
the morning.

Picking a day is a choice, and it shapes the picture. A scene like this shows one day well
and says nothing about the other 364.

## The sun

The light follows the real sun. Its height and direction come from the date, the time and
the place, with the short formulas of the kind the Astronomical Almanac gives for an
approximate solar position [@michalsky1988] (from `data.js`):

```js
function sunPosition(utcMs, latDeg, lonDeg) {
  const rad = Math.PI / 180;
  const d = utcMs / 86400000 + 2440587.5 - 2451545.0; // days since J2000.0
  const g = (357.529 + 0.98560028 * d) * rad;
  const q = 280.459 + 0.98564736 * d;
  const L = (q + 1.915 * Math.sin(g) + 0.020 * Math.sin(2 * g)) * rad;
  const e = (23.439 - 0.00000036 * d) * rad;
  const ra = Math.atan2(Math.cos(e) * Math.sin(L), Math.cos(L));
  const dec = Math.asin(Math.sin(e) * Math.sin(L));
  const gmst = (18.697374558 + 24.06570982441908 * d) % 24;
  const ha = ((gmst * 15 + lonDeg) * rad) - ra;
  const lat = latDeg * rad;
  const el = Math.asin(Math.sin(lat) * Math.sin(dec) + Math.cos(lat) * Math.cos(dec) * Math.cos(ha));
  const az = Math.atan2(-Math.sin(ha), Math.tan(dec) * Math.cos(lat) - Math.sin(lat) * Math.cos(ha));
  return { el, az: (az + 2 * Math.PI) % (2 * Math.PI) };
}
```

The result is good to about a degree, which is plenty for light and shadow. The colours of
sky and sunlight are then read from small hand-made tables by the sun's height; they are
drawn, not measured.

## The ground

The heights are Geoscience Australia's one-arc-second elevation model, about 30 m between
points, which goes back to the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission [@farr2007]. For the mesh
the scene averages them to 297 by 180 cells of about 210 by 250 m and draws them three
times higher than life. The coastline, roads, railway and power lines are from
OpenStreetMap [@haklay2008], thinned with the line-simplification method of
@douglas1973.

A mesh of 210 m cells is too coarse to show a ridge. So the light does not use the mesh's
slopes but the slopes of the full 30 m heights, stored as a picture: two colour channels
hold the direction the ground faces, the third how open a spot is (from
`diorama_imagery.py`):

```python
gz, gx = np.gradient(hs, dz, dx)
n = np.dstack([-gx * VEX, np.ones_like(hs), -gz * VEX])
n /= np.linalg.norm(n, axis=2, keepdims=True)
# openness: height against its surroundings (about 400 m around), 0.5 = level ground
op = np.clip(0.5 + (hs - blur(hs, 13.0)) / 70.0, 0, 1)
```

Lighting a coarse surface with finer slopes kept in a picture goes back to @blinn1978.
The third channel dims the light from the sky a little in valleys and raises it on ridges.

### Shadows of the hills

When the sun is low, the Flinders Ranges throw long shadows over the plain. The scene works
them out on the processor, not on the graphics chip: from every ground cell it walks toward
the sun over the drawn heights and checks whether the ground rises above the ray (from
`main.js`):

```js
          let x = gx, z = gy, ray = HY[i] + 0.004, v = 1;
          for (let k = 1; k <= maxSteps; k++) {
            x += dx; z += dz; ray += dh;
            if (ray > maxHY || x < 0 || z < 0 || x >= nx - 1 || z >= ny - 1) break;
            // the ground under the ray, between the four cells around it
            const ix = x | 0, iz = z | 0, fx = x - ix, fz = z - iz, o = iz * nx + ix;
            const g = (HY[o] * (1 - fx) + HY[o + 1] * fx) * (1 - fz) + (HY[o + nx] * (1 - fx) + HY[o + nx + 1] * fx) * fz;
            const d = g - ray;
            if (d > 0) {
              const t = d / (0.006 + 0.05 * k * step);
              if (t >= 1) { v = 0; break; }
              if (1 - t < v) v = 1 - t;
            }
          }
          out[i] = v * 255;
```

![The walk toward the sun, seen from the side. The edge of a shadow is soft because a cell
that is only just covered gets a value between light and dark.](fig-shadow.svg){#fig-shadow fig-alt="Side view of a hill with a sun ray from a ground cell: steps along the ray, the hill above the ray"}

The walk is redone when the sun has moved more than 0.4 degrees, 24 rows of cells per
frame, so that it never holds up a frame. Because the hills are drawn three times higher,
their shadows are longer than in life. The two classic ways to draw shadows on a graphics
chip, shadow volumes and shadow maps, go back to @crow1977 and @williams1978; for a single
height field and one sun, the walk above is simpler than either.

## The satellite picture

The ground's colour is a picture from the Sentinel-2B satellite [@drusch2012], taken on
15 October 2026 at about 11:30 local time, one day before the replayed day, with no clouds
over the area. I use it as Geoscience Australia publishes it in Digital Earth Australia
[@dhu2017]: corrected for the atmosphere, for the angles of sun and view and for the slope
of the ground, a method described for Landsat pictures in @li2012. What arrives is not a photograph but, for every
10 m of ground, the share of red, green and blue light the surface reflects. One line
turns that into a colour for the eye, the same for all three channels (from
`diorama_imagery.py`):

```python
disp = np.clip(rgb * 3.2, 0, 1) ** (1 / 2.0)      # the same exposure and gamma as ground.jpg
```

The picture carries the light of its own moment: shadows in it point where the sun stood at
11:30. The scene mixes that with its drawn light of the hour, which is a compromise, not a
correction.

### Out to the horizon

At first the scene was a block of 62 by 44 km standing in empty space, and on a phone it
looked like a brown slab. The same satellite pass covers far more, so the land now runs on
to the horizon in three rings of falling detail.

![Three rings around one centre. The picture gets coarser with distance, and beyond about
100 km the land dissolves into the colour of the horizon.](fig-rings.svg){#fig-rings fig-alt="Nested rectangles: the scene of 62 by 44 km, a middle ring of 115 by 111 km, the wider land of 229 by 222 km, with small squares for the detail tiles"}

The shader for the wider land looks up the coarse picture everywhere and lays the sharper
one over it where it exists (from `shaders.js`):

```glsl
  vec2 um = vP.xz / (uOuterBox * 0.5) + 0.5;
  vec2 em = abs(um - 0.5);
  float inMid = uMidMix * (1.0 - smoothstep(0.47, 0.5, max(em.x, em.y)));
  picture = mix(picture, texture(uMid, um).rgb, inMid);
  mask = mix(mask, texture(uMidWater, um).r, inMid);
```

Far out, the land fades into the colour the sky has at the horizon in that direction, so
there is no edge to see:

```glsl
  float far = smoothstep(50.0, 106.0, length(vP.xz));
  vec3 v = normalize(vP - uEye);
  o = vec4(mix(fogged(c, vP), tone(horizonAt(v) + veilAt(v)), far), uOuterMix);
```

Rings of detail that get coarser with distance are the idea of geometry clipmaps
[@losasso2004]. There the rings follow the viewer; here they are fixed around the place,
which is enough for a scene one looks at from all sides but does not travel through.

### Where the water is

The first shore was the OpenStreetMap coastline on the 210 m mesh, which gave steps. The
satellite sees water directly: water reflects some green light and almost no near
infrared. The normalized difference of the two is a water index that has been in use since
@mcfeeters1996 (from `diorama_imagery.py`):

```python
green, nir = band_mosaic("nbart_green"), band_mosaic(NIR)
index = (green - nir) / np.maximum(green + nir, 1e-4)
water = (index > 0.0) & (nir < 0.12)
```

The scene keeps the result only on low ground, so that a dark hill shadow cannot count as
water, and draws moving water exactly there: the gulf, the river arm through the town, a
few ponds. The shore is therefore the shore of that satellite moment, at whatever the tide
was then.

## Fine detail, from an older picture

Ten to fifteen metres per pixel is coarse when the view comes close to a street. I found no
openly licensed colour picture of this area at a finer scale. There is an older black-and-white
one: Geoscience Australia's reference image at 2.5 m, put together from pictures taken
between 2006 and 2010; the mosaic used here is from 2008 to 2010.

It is older than the wind and solar farms, so it cannot replace the picture of 2026. But
most of what is fine in a landscape changes slowly: creek lines, rock ledges, the street
grid. So the scene takes from the old picture only what is finer than about 10 m. Each
tile holds the picture divided by its own blur (from `diorama_detail.py`):

```python
soft = blur(pic, BLUR_PX)
ratio = np.where(soft > 14, pic / np.maximum(soft, 1.0), 1.0)    # dark water and no-data: no detail
```

Close up, the shader multiplies the colour of 2026 by that pattern of lighter and darker
(from `shaders.js`):

```glsl
  if (fine > 0.0) sat *= mix(1.0, uDetRange.x + uDetRange.y * dv / max(k0 + k1 + k2 + k3, 1e-3), fine);
```

Putting the fine structure of a sharp black-and-white picture onto a coarser colour
picture is called pansharpening; @vivone2015 compare the methods. What I do is a crude
relative of it: the two pictures are from different satellites and about sixteen to eighteen years apart.
Where something was built in between, the fine structure is wrong. On the solar farms the
scene therefore leaves it out, and the info panel says so for the rest.

The old picture comes as one file of 410 GB in a map projection, the transverse Mercator
[@karney2011], and the script reads only the rows and columns it needs. The scene's box is
cut into 6 by 5 tiles of 4096 by 3520 pixels; the 11 tiles with the town and the plants
are built, 18 MB in all, and the page fetches tiles only when the view is closer than 16 km
to the ground it looks at.

![The town from the west at 09:00: the river arm from the water outline, streets and blocks
from the fine detail, houses from open outlines.](shot-town.jpg){#fig-town fig-alt="Oblique view of a small town on both sides of a narrow water arm, with a bridge, many small houses and sun glitter on the water"}

## Houses

OpenStreetMap has 558 buildings in the area, a small part of the town. Most houses come from a second open source: outlines that a model at Microsoft found
in satellite and aerial pictures. That gives 11,204 more. Model output has errors: some
houses are missing, some are merged, some sit a little off.

Where both sources have the same building it must be drawn once. Two outlines count as the
same when the centre of one lies inside the other, or when they cover more than 30 % of
the smaller one; the cover is measured by sampling (from `diorama_buildings.py`):

```python
def share(a, b, ba, bb, n: int = 24) -> float:
    """Overlap of two outlines as a share of the smaller one (sampled on a grid)."""
    x0, y0, x1, y1 = max(ba[0], bb[0]), max(ba[1], bb[1]), min(ba[2], bb[2]), min(ba[3], bb[3])
    if x1 <= x0 or y1 <= y0:
        return 0.0
    hits = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            p = (x0 + (i + 0.5) * (x1 - x0) / n, y0 + (j + 0.5) * (y1 - y0) / n)
            hits += inside(p, a) and inside(p, b)
    return hits * (x1 - x0) * (y1 - y0) / n / n / min(ring_area(a), ring_area(b))
```

In the page every outline is raised into a house on its true outline and drawn half again
as high. Small, nearly rectangular
outlines get a hipped roof, and for that the code needs the best-fitting rectangle. It
tries the direction of every edge and keeps the smallest (from `scene.js`):

```js
  const fitBox = (P) => {
    let best = null;
    for (let i = 0; i < P.length; i++) {
      const a = P[i], b = P[(i + 1) % P.length], len = Math.hypot(b[0] - a[0], b[1] - a[1]);
      if (len < 0.5) continue;
      const ux = (b[0] - a[0]) / len, uz = (b[1] - a[1]) / len;
      let s0 = 1e9, s1 = -1e9, t0 = 1e9, t1 = -1e9;
      for (const q of P) { const sv = q[0] * ux + q[1] * uz, tv = -q[0] * uz + q[1] * ux; s0 = Math.min(s0, sv); s1 = Math.max(s1, sv); t0 = Math.min(t0, tv); t1 = Math.max(t1, tv); }
      const ar = (s1 - s0) * (t1 - t0);
      if (!best || ar < best.ar) best = { ar, ux, uz, s0, s1, t0, t1 };
    }
```

For a convex outline the smallest rectangle around it does have one side along one of its
edges [@freeman1975]; for an outline with a notch, trying its edges is a good guess, not a
proof. The other buildings get a flat roof on their true outline, cut into triangles by
snipping off one "ear" after another, which works because every simple polygon has ears
[@meisters1975].

The Microsoft data also carry an estimated height. Here its median is 1.0 m, less than a
door, so the page uses it only from 3 m up and makes up the rest by size and kind. Walls,
windows and doors are a pattern drawn in the shader; the roofs take part of their colour
from the satellite picture at that spot.

## Turbines, panels, battery

The 109 turbines stand where OpenStreetMap maps them, with hub height and rotor size from
the same source, drawn about five times larger than life. A rotor turns faster the more
its farm gives, as a share of the farm's maximum capacity in the operator's registration
list. That is a drawing of output. It is not the real rotor speed, and the direction the
rotors face is not in the data.

Bungala's panels are listed as tracking in the registration list, so the scene turns the
rows with the sun, drawn as single-axis trackers; the rows are several times wider and
farther apart than real ones. A
field's glow shows its output share. The battery is eight containers; their number is a
drawing, not a count. It glows blue while it takes power in and amber while it gives power
out.

![Among the turbines of Lincoln Gap at 16:30. The shadows of towers and blades are the
turbine's own shape, slid along the sun's ray onto the ground.](shot-lincoln.jpg){#fig-lincoln fig-alt="Close view of white wind turbines on rocky hills with long shadows"}

## Light, haze, water and glow

A handful of small ideas make the picture read as a landscape and not as a diagram.

**Highlights that roll off.** Bright ground under a high sun would clip to white. A short
curve leaves everything below a knee as it is and bends what is above toward 1 (from
`shaders.js`):

```glsl
vec3 tone(vec3 c) {
  vec3 over = max(c - 0.6, 0.0);
  return min(c, 0.6) + 0.4 * (1.0 - exp(-over * 2.5));
}
```

Mapping a wide range of light into the narrow range of a screen is the subject of
@reinhard2002; my curve is a small stand-in for what they describe.

**Water.** Water mirrors the sky at flat angles and shows its own colour when one looks
straight down. The share of mirror follows Schlick's approximation [@schlick1994]; the
water's own colour is the satellite picture's (from `shaders.js`):

```glsl
  float fr = 0.03 + 0.97 * pow(1.0 - max(dot(n, v), 0.0), 5.0);
  vec3 deep = vec3(0.05, 0.22, 0.30);
  // the water's own colour from the satellite picture: shallows, channels, tidal flats
  deep = mix(deep, picture * 0.8, pictureMix);
  deep = mix(deep, vec3(0.01, 0.03, 0.06), uNight);
  // it is lit by the sky and by the sun's brightness, not its tint
  float sunLum = dot(uSunCol, vec3(0.3, 0.55, 0.15));
  vec3 body = deep * (uAmb * 1.3 + vec3(sunLum) * 0.5 * (0.4 + 0.6 * gVis));
  vec3 c = mix(body, skyAt(reflect(-v, n), uHorizon, uZenith), fr * 0.75);
```

On top comes the sun's glitter, a sharp highlight of the kind @phong1975 introduced, broken
up by a moving noise pattern.

**Shadows of houses and turbines.** Each is the object itself, slid along the sun's ray
down to the ground (from `shaders.js`):

```glsl
  if (uMode == 1) {
    float g = textureLod(uHeight, p.xz / uBox + 0.5, 0.0).r;
    p.xz -= uSun.xz * (max(p.y - g, 0.0) / max(uSun.y, 0.2));
    p.y = textureLod(uHeight, p.xz / uBox + 0.5, 0.0).r + 0.0008;
  }
```

Where two such shadows overlap, the ground must not get twice as dark. A stencil lets
every pixel be darkened once (from `main.js`):

```js
    gl.enable(gl.STENCIL_TEST); gl.stencilFunc(gl.EQUAL, 0, 0xff); gl.stencilOp(gl.KEEP, gl.KEEP, gl.INCR);
```

**Glow.** After the frame is drawn, its brightest parts are copied, shrunk to a quarter,
blurred and laid back over the picture. That gives the sun on the water and the windows at
night their soft halo.

## Staying fast on a phone

**Load late.** The page starts with the day's numbers, the mesh and drawn colours. The
satellite pictures, the fine slopes, the water outline and the houses arrive afterwards
and fade in.

**Load only what is near.** The 2.5 m tiles are fetched when the view comes close and let
go when it leaves (from `main.js`):

```js
    if (cam.dist < 16) {
      // the two by two tiles around the point the view turns about
      const i0 = Math.floor((cam.target[0] + place.Wk / 2) / tw - 0.5), j0 = Math.floor((cam.target[2] + place.Hk / 2) / th - 0.5);
      for (const j of [j0, j0 + 1]) for (const i of [i0, i0 + 1]) if (inf.tiles.includes(i + '_' + j)) want.push(i + '_' + j);
    } else if (cam.dist > 26) {
      detail.slots.forEach((sl, k) => { if (sl) { gl.deleteTexture(sl.tex); detail.slots[k] = null; } });
    }
```

**Build in pieces.** Raising 11,762 outlines into about 580,000 corner points takes a
moment. The page does it 600 houses at a time between frames. The hill shadows are spread
over frames in the same way.

**Resolution that gives way.** If the frame rate stays low, the picture is drawn with fewer
pixels and scaled up (from `main.js`):

```js
      if (animate && now - motion.t0 > 9000 && diag.fps >= 8 && diag.fps < 27 && scale > 0.55) { if (++slow >= 2) { scale *= 0.85; slow = 0; resize(); } } else slow = 0;
```

The page also stops when the tab is hidden, and shows a still picture with a play button
for visitors who have asked their device for less motion.

## The battery game

The scene has one thing to do besides looking: run the battery yourself. The day passes in
two minutes, the price line ends at "now", and three keys decide what the battery does:
charge, hold, discharge.

![The game on a phone: the price line stops at the present moment.](shot-game.jpg){#fig-game width="50%" fig-alt="Phone screenshot at night with a price panel whose line has just begun, and a panel with a battery bar, a money figure and three buttons"}

The rules fit in one function. Charging buys energy from the grid at the price of the
interval, discharging sells it, and some is lost on the way in and on the way out (from
`game.js`):

```js
  function step(level, mode, price) {
    if (price == null || mode === 0) return { level, money: 0, mw: 0, mwh: 0 };
    if (mode > 0) {
      const bought = Math.max(0, Math.min(pIn * h, (cap - level) / eff));
      return { level: level + bought * eff, money: -bought * price, mw: -bought / h, mwh: bought };
    }
    const sold = Math.max(0, Math.min(pOut * h, level * eff));
    return { level: level - sold / eff, money: sold * price, mw: sold / h, mwh: sold };
  }
```

The size of the battery and how fast it can charge and discharge are from the operator's
registration list. That 85 % of the energy bought comes back out is my assumption, not a
figure for this battery. There are no fees and no other income, the battery starts empty,
and what is left in it at the end counts for nothing.

At the end the game shows three figures. The second is the best any plan with these three
keys could have made, knowing every price in advance. It is found by working backwards
from the end of the day: for the last interval the best choice at every level of charge is
obvious, and from there each earlier interval can be solved in turn (from `game.js`):

```js
    for (let i = n - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
      const cur = new Float64Array(L + 1), pick = new Int8Array(L + 1);
      for (let j = 0; j <= L; j++) {
        let top = -Infinity;
        for (const mode of [0, 1, -1]) {
          const r = step(lev(j), mode, prices[i]);
          const v = r.money + at(next, r.level);
          if (v > top + 1e-9) { top = v; pick[j] = mode; }
        }
        cur[j] = top;
      }
      choice[i] = pick;
      next = cur;
    }
```

This is dynamic programming [@bellman1954]. On this day the best plan makes about
A$1,677; @fig-day shows when it charges and when it sells. The third figure is what the
real battery's five-minute readings come to at the same prices, about A$20. It is an
estimate from readings, not what the plant was paid, and batteries can also be paid for
services that keep the grid steady, which these data do not show.

What storage can earn from price differences alone has been estimated properly, with
several years of prices, for a market in the United States [@sioshansi2009] and for
this one, in South Australia [@mcconnell2015]. The game is a what-if on one day. It is not a valuation, and
nothing in it is advice.

## What the scene leaves out

One day, chosen for the picture it gives. No clouds, no weather, no wind direction. Only the five plants with
their own data are drawn; smaller solar plants nearby are left out. The scene says nothing
about what a plant earns, why its output was low, or how reliable it is. Building heights,
roofs and walls are mostly made up, and the Microsoft outlines were not checked one by one.
The fine ground detail is about sixteen to eighteen years older than the colours. The hills, and with them
their shadows, are three times too high. The picture is replayed, not live.

## A reading guide

**The market and what storage is worth.** The falling market value of wind and solar
[@hirth2013]; wind and prices in South Australia [@cutler2011]; the stability of the
market [@simshauser2018]; the value of storage [@sioshansi2009; @mcconnell2015].

**Pictures from orbit.** The Sentinel-2 mission [@drusch2012]; Digital Earth Australia
[@dhu2017] and the correction behind its pictures [@li2012]; water from green and
near-infrared light [@mcfeeters1996]; pansharpening [@vivone2015].

**Heights and maps.** The radar mission behind the heights [@farr2007]; OpenStreetMap
[@haklay2008]; simplifying lines [@douglas1973]; the transverse Mercator projection
[@karney2011]; the sun's position [@michalsky1988].

**Shapes.** Ears of polygons [@meisters1975]; the smallest rectangle around a shape
[@freeman1975].

**Drawing.** Shading [@phong1975; @schlick1994]; slopes kept in a picture [@blinn1978];
shadows [@crow1977; @williams1978]; rings of detail [@losasso2004]; light for a screen
[@reinhard2002].

**Deciding step by step.** Dynamic programming [@bellman1954].
