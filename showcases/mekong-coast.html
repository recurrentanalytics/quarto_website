Landsat dry seasons from 1988 to 2025 over five stretches of the delta’s coast: where the mangrove edge retreats, where it grows, why, and what a dyke or a mangrove belt can and cannot do.
Published

November 19, 2026

Showcase 3 of 4 · policy and decisions

The Mekong Delta feeds a country and keeps losing its edge to the sea. Satellites have watched it since the 1980s. Here is what they show, why it happens, and what the choice between a dyke and a mangrove belt really depends on.

See it

Every clear dry season from 1988 to 2025, five boxes of 9 × 9 km on the delta’s coast, as Landsat saw them. The bright line is the seaward edge of vegetation, mostly mangrove, found by the same rule every year. The dashed line is where it was in the first clear years.

edge this year first clear years

How this was measured. Landsat 5, 7, 8 and 9 surface reflectance, December to April (the dry season), up to five of the clearest, least hazy scenes per year, cloud and shadow removed, per-pixel median. Along transects every 90 m, the edge is the first point, coming from the sea, where the vegetation index (NDVI 0.3 or more, with bright near-infrared) stays high for 75 m. We use the vegetation edge, not the waterline: on these mudflats the waterline moves a long way with the tide, the mangrove edge does not. What it cannot see: a dyke stops the edge from moving, so “no change” in front of a dyke is not “stable”; new planting shows up as growth; pixels are 30 m, so a single year’s step of less than about 30 m is noise. The rate is a trend per transect (Theil–Sen, which a few bad years cannot drag), then the median over the transects in the box; the range shows the middle half of them.

Why

The coast is made of mud the river brings. Less mud arrives, the land sinks, the sea rises, and the mangroves that hold the mud are cut or squeezed. Each driver has a number, and the studies disagree, so here are ranges.

Mud supply · dams

≈ 167 → 43–99 Mt / yr

Suspended sediment reaching the delta. One study puts it at about 167 Mt/yr before the dams and 43 Mt/yr in 2012–2015, a 74% drop. Another finds about 99 Mt/yr still arriving at the delta's head, the loss so far mostly from dams in China, and warns that planned dams in Laos and Cambodia could halve it.

Binh et al. 2020, Geomorphology 353; Bussi et al. 2021, Sci. Total Environ. 755.

Sand mining

≈ 8–20 million m³ / yr

Sand dredged from the rivers of Vietnam's delta, by earlier estimates; the official figure for 2018 alone is 17.8 million m³, and researchers say official numbers miss informal mining. Along the lower Mekong, about 50 million tonnes of sand a year are taken out, against about 6 million tonnes the river carries into the delta.

Jordan et al. 2019, Sci. Rep. 9; Hackney et al. 2020, Nat. Sustain. 3.

Subsidence

≈ 1–4 cm / yr

The land sinks, much of it because groundwater is pumped. About 1.1 cm/yr on average from pumping alone, locally more than 2.5; satellite radar shows 1–4 cm/yr over large areas. The delta is on average only 0.82 m above sea level.

Minderhoud et al. 2017, Environ. Res. Lett. 12; Erban et al. 2014, Environ. Res. Lett. 9; Minderhoud et al. 2019, Nat. Commun. 10.

Sea-level rise

2.2 vs 5.6–13.5 mm / yr

At the stable gauge of Vung Tau the sea rose 2.2 mm/yr (1987–2015). At gauges on the delta coast the relative rise is 5.6 to 13.5 mm/yr: the difference is the land going down.

Nguyen, Schwarzer & Ricklefs 2023, Estuar. Coast. Shelf Sci. 283.

Mangroves

≈ 140 m belt

On the east coast a mangrove belt needs at least about 140 m to stay healthy; dykes built too close squeeze it. But once the mud in front is gone, a wider belt does not stop the retreat. Even the global mangrove maps disagree on whether Vietnam's mangroves grew or shrank between 1996 and 2020.

Phan et al. 2015, J. Coastal Res. 31(2); Besset et al. 2019, Estuar. Coast. Shelf Sci. 226; Global Mangrove Watch v3 and v4.1.

Not only the dams. Erosion on the Ca Mau coast started long before the big dams. Canals and rice fields have trapped the river’s mud on land since the early 20th century (Tamura et al. 2020, Sci. Rep. 10:8085). For 2003–2012, Anthony et al. (2015) found more than half of the delta’s shoreline, which is over 600 km long, eroding. On the 180 km muddy East Sea coast nearly 90% retreated; the mean rate there rose from about 6 m a year (2003–2007) to more than 12 m (2007–2012), with more than 50 m a year in places.

Decide

Hold the line with a dyke, or let a mangrove belt hold the mud? The evidence says it depends on one thing first: is there still mud in front of the coast?

Mud still there, room to grow

Mangrove belt

Mangroves calm waves and trap mud. For small waves, up to about half a metre, a belt can be two to five times cheaper than a submerged breakwater (global review). It needs width, at least about 140 m on the east coast, and a dyke set far enough back to leave that room.

Narayan et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11(5); Phan et al. 2015.

Mud gone, waves at the trees

Fences and breakwaters first

Where erosion has destroyed the belt, mangroves can only come back after the waves are cut. Bamboo fences do that, slow the erosion and let mud settle again; then the mangroves can be restored.

Albers & Schmitt 2015, Wetl. Ecol. Manag. 23.

Valuable land, no room

Dyke

A dyke holds a line, but a belt built too close in front of it gets squeezed. The strongest protection puts the two in series: a mangrove belt on the mudflat in front, a sound dyke behind.

Phan et al. 2015; Albers & Schmitt 2015.

What it costs: we don’t show a number. We found no open, like-for-like comparison of the cost per kilometre of a dyke and a mangrove belt in the delta. The figures in the press cover different works and periods, so dividing them would mislead.

Vietnam’s government made working with nature, not against it, a guiding principle for the delta in 2017 (Resolution 120/NQ-CP, in our words). The satellite record above shows where that is hardest: where the mud has already gone.
