Hurricane Maria 2017 over Puerto Rico's power grid

We sent Maria across a model of the island's transmission grid, hour by hour. Then we held the model up against the satellite and the outage reports. It gets the direction right and misses the depth. Here is the likely reason.

LiveLines kernel 865bcc9 · preset chain_maria_pr · 8 runs, seed 42 · hours in UTC

The grid

56 substations and 92 branches (76 lines, 16 transformers) at 115 and 230 kV, built from OpenStreetMap with PyPSA-Earth, plus the island's 2017 power plants.

Behind it: the island's lights on normal nights before the storms (NASA Black Marble).

The storm

Maria made landfall in the southeast at 10:15 UTC on 20 September 2017 (06:15 local): category 4, 135 kt. It crossed the whole island and left in the northwest.

Hour by hour

Scroll to run the clock. A line turns red once it is down in at least half of the 8 runs, amber while only some.

The first lines break at hour 19. Each section of line has an even chance of breaking at a gust of about 79 m/s; a line fails at its first broken section. The strongest gust the model puts on any line: 88.9 m/s.

Closest approach: hour 24, 20 Sep 13:00 UTC · 09:00 local. After that the wind breaks nothing new, and nothing gets repaired: the model has no repair crews.

48 hours on

The wind has broken 39 to 45 of 92 branches, depending on the run. The model still serves 39% of the island's load (median of the runs), down from 99%.

So far, the direction is right: a severe outage.

Island total, weighted by load: 35% to 57% (5th to 95th percentile of the runs); 47% with an ideal operator. The DC power flow trips up to 6 more branches on overload.

What the satellite saw

Every one of the 50 substation areas with satellite data fell below half its normal light. On average they kept 19%.

The model keeps the same areas at 63% served; only 17 drop below half. Nor can it say which areas went darkest (rank correlation 0.08).

A plain mean over the areas, so higher than the load-weighted island figure.

What was reported

On 24 September the U.S. Department of Energy reported 1,569,796 customers without power: 100%, confirmed.

Three days earlier, at its last hour, the model still served 39% of the island's load. The same miss as in the night lights.

Customers are not load; the model has no repair, so its last hour stands for 24 September.

Why it misses

The model breaks lines and reroutes power around the gaps; whatever still connects to a running plant stays lit. The real grid failed as a system: the whole island went dark.

The likely cause, as LiveLines' scoreboard names it: no system-wide collapse in the model. A first collapse rule (a cut-off part of the grid goes dark when its sudden shortfall exceeds 25% of its load) brings the model to 57%; with over-frequency trips, 55%. Closer, still far from 19%.

The comparison points to the collapse; it does not prove it. Distribution damage (poles, lines, transformers), not modelled, adds to the gap.

The scorecard

Mean over the 50 substation areas with satellite data: share of normal night light (satellite) against share of load served at the model's last hour.

Hours in the model, months on the island

The model's end state, 61% of load not served, is where the island stood only weeks later, in its slow repair. The model has no repair, so this comparison runs one way only.

Customers without power as the DOE situation reports state them (from early October estimated, not counted). Load not restored = 100% minus the share of normal peak load restored that the reports give (2,685 MW in October and November; "generally between 2500 and 3000 MW" in December and January, recalibrated from 3 January; on 28 March "of pre-storm capacity"). Customers are not load, the model's share is of its own modelled load, and the model does not see distribution lines.

Limits, in plain words

  • Transmission only. The model stops at the substation. The lines to streets and homes, where much of Maria's damage was, are not in it.
  • Today's map, not 2017's. The grid comes from today's OpenStreetMap, and keeps 1,415 of the 2,659 km of overhead line mapped there. Line ratings are standard values per voltage, not the real ones.
  • Plants and demand. The 2017 plants are at nameplate, with their status from a 2025 tracker; 340 MW of them may already have been out of service in 2017. Small plants and hydro are missing. Demand is modelled, not measured.
  • Borrowed fragility. How likely a line is to fail in a given gust comes from a study of Indian transmission towers. Nothing is repaired, and there are only 8 runs.
  • Light is not power. Night lights measure emitted light. Each area is simply the land nearest a substation, not what that substation really feeds. There were no usable satellite nights until 27 September; the satellite numbers rest on 27 and 28 September.
  • Different moments. The model covers 48 hours around landfall; the satellite nights are 7 and 8 days after landfall, the DOE count of 24 September 4 days after. Without repair, the model's last hour stands for those dates.
  • One loose end. One substation carries load but has no line in the model: it is dark from hour 0. That is why the intact grid serves 99%, not 100%.
  • Collapse rules are first drafts. Their thresholds come from North American (NERC) standards; Puerto Rico is not under NERC, and the values are placeholders.
  • A screening model, not a forecast. Every module in this chain is at placeholder maturity.
